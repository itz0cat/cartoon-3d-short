# "The Fall That Slowed Down Time" — Original Script

**Format**: 45 s (~116 words at ~2.7-3.0 words/s), 720x1280 @ 30 fps (1350 frames).
**Topic**: The subjective slow-motion illusion during life-threatening free fall.
**Scientific Facts & Sources**:
- In 2007, neuroscientist David Eagleman conducted an experiment to test whether human time dilation during terrifying events is actual high-speed visual perception or a memory illusion.
- Subjects were dropped backwards 150 feet (46 m) in free fall at 70 mph into a net suspended at the Zero Gravity Thrill City in Dallas, Texas.
- Subjects wore a "perceptual chronometer" on their wrists with alternating numbers flashing at rates faster than the normal human critical flicker fusion threshold.
- None of the subjects could read the digits during the fall, proving the visual cortex does not run at higher frames per second.
- The amygdala triggers high-density memory storage under fear, making the event feel twice as long in retrospective recall. (Source: Eagleman et al., PLoS ONE 2007, "Does Time Really Slow Down during a Frightening Event?").

## Narration Script

If you fall off a tower, your brain thinks you entered The Matrix.
In 2007, scientists dropped volunteers a hundred and fifty feet into free fall.
While falling at seventy miles an hour, they stared at wristwatches flashing numbers.
If panic actually slowed down time, reading them would be easy.
Nobody could read a single one.
Luckily, the net caught them.
Unluckily, their brains were lying.
Panic does not speed up your eyes.
Instead, terror triggers an emergency memory dump, recording every tiny detail without compression.
When you land, your brain unzips that massive file and assumes the fall lasted ten seconds.
The world never slowed down.
You just took five times longer to remember screaming.
